<h2>What Is Rent Stabilization in NYC (and How Is It Different from Rent Control)?</h2><p>Rent stabilization is a system of rent regulation that limits how much landlords can raise rent each year and guarantees tenants the right to renew their lease. It covers roughly one million apartments across New York City, making it one of the largest regulated housing systems in the country [1]. Rent control, by contrast, is a much smaller and older program that applies to a shrinking number of apartments where the tenant (or a qualifying family member) has lived continuously since before July 1971 [2]. If you own a small rental portfolio in NYC, there's a good chance you're dealing with rent stabilization rather than rent control, since rent control units are increasingly rare and tied to specific long-term tenancies.</p><h2>How to Tell If Your Property Is Rent-Stabilized</h2><p>Generally, an apartment is rent-stabilized if it's in a building with six or more units built before 1974 [2]. However, there are exceptions in both directions: buildings that received tax incentives like 421-a or J-51 may be stabilized even if built later, while co-ops, condos, and owner-occupied small buildings are often exempt. The most reliable way to confirm status is to pull the building's registration history directly from the New York State Division of Housing and Community Renewal (DHCR), which maintains public records of every registered rent-stabilized unit [1]. If you inherited a property or bought a multi-family building without a clear paper trail, this is one of the first things to verify before setting rent or signing a lease.</p><h2>Key Rules Every Small Landlord Must Follow</h2><p>Once a unit is confirmed stabilized, several rules kick in automatically:</p><ul><li><strong>Rent increases:</strong> You can only raise rent by the percentage set annually by the Rent Guidelines Board (RGB), not whatever the market will bear.</li><li><strong>Lease renewals:</strong> Tenants have the right to renew their lease for either a one- or two-year term, and you must offer this renewal in writing within the proper timeframe.</li><li><strong>Succession rights:</strong> Certain family members who lived with the tenant for the required period may have the legal right to take over the lease after the tenant moves out or passes away [6].</li></ul><p>Failing to follow any one of these rules — even unintentionally — can expose a small landlord to tenant complaints, rent overcharge claims, or litigation that drags on for months.</p><h2>Allowable Rent Increases: Understanding the Rent Guidelines Board (RGB)</h2><p>The RGB meets each year to set the maximum percentage increase landlords may apply to one- and two-year lease renewals for stabilized units [3]. These percentages change annually based on operating costs, inflation, and public hearings, and they are not negotiable on a unit-by-unit basis. Unlike market-rate rentals, you cannot simply decide to raise rent 8% because comparable units in the neighborhood are renting higher. Missing an RGB order or misapplying an increase is one of the most common — and most expensive — mistakes small landlords make, since overcharges can trigger refunds, penalties, and treble damages if found willful.</p><h2>What You Can't Do: Common Compliance Mistakes That Lead to Fines or Lawsuits</h2><p>Some of the most frequent violations small landlords commit include:</p><ul><li>Charging rent above the legal regulated amount without proper DHCR approval for Individual Apartment Improvements (IAIs)</li><li>Failing to offer a timely, correct lease renewal</li><li>Not registering the apartment's rent annually with DHCR [5]</li><li>Attempting to remove a unit from stabilization without following the strict deregulation rules</li><li>Ignoring a tenant's valid succession rights claim</li></ul><p>Tenants can file a rent overcharge complaint going back several years, and DHCR or housing court can order refunds plus interest if a landlord is found to have overcharged [7]. For a small landlord managing one or two buildings without legal staff, these mistakes are often made out of simple unfamiliarity with the rules rather than intent — but that doesn't reduce the financial exposure.</p><h2>Evictions and Non-Renewals Under Rent Stabilization: What's Legal</h2><p>Rent stabilization significantly limits a landlord's ability to evict or decline to renew a lease compared to market-rate housing. Legal grounds for non-renewal are narrow and generally include the owner's intent to use the unit as a primary residence, nonpayment of rent, lease violations, or nuisance behavior — each of which requires specific notice and, often, a court proceeding.
